Chapter 1

Start here

Most students don’t have a study problem. They have a study method problem. You sit down, you stare at the notes, you re-read the chapter — and three days later, it’s gone. That’s not a laziness issue. That’s a technique issue.

The most dangerous studying myth is that time spent equals learning achieved.

The science of learning — called cognitive science — has been studying how memory actually works for decades. The gap between what works and what most students do is enormous. This guide closes that gap.

Myths that waste your time

  • Re-reading your notes is studying. Re-reading creates familiarity, not memory. You feel like you know it because it looks familiar, but you haven’t actually encoded it. A major review of study techniques rated re-reading and highlighting as low-utility: they feel productive, but they don’t reliably improve test performance (Dunlosky et al., 2013).
  • Highlighting is a productive study habit. Highlighting feels active but is almost entirely passive. You’re selecting, not processing. It becomes useful only if you then do something with what you highlighted.
  • Cramming the night before works. Cramming can genuinely help for a test in the morning — massed practice does load short-term memory. The catch: you forget it almost as fast, so it’s useless for the final three weeks later or the foundation you’ll need next year. For anything you need to keep, spacing wins decisively.
  • You need to study in one long session. Shorter, focused bursts with breaks outperform marathon sessions. Fatigue degrades encoding and attention — your brain isn’t storing what you’re “reviewing” if you’re running on empty.
  • You have a fixed “learning style” and should only study in it. This is one of the most popular ideas in education — and one of the least supported. When researchers reviewed the evidence, they found almost no well-designed studies showing that matching teaching to someone’s preferred “style” helps, and several that found it doesn’t (Pashler et al., 2008). What is true: some content is naturally visual (a map) or verbal (a poem), and using several formats strengthens memory for everyone — not because of your “type,” but because each format adds another route back to the memory.
  • You can study fine with your phone next to you. Every glance at a notification forces a costly mental “reload” back into the task, and a phone within reach is a constant temptation to check it. One well-known study found that even a silent, face-down phone on the desk lowered scores on working-memory tests (Ward et al., 2017); a later replication didn’t find that effect (Ruiz Pardo & Minda, 2022), but the interruptions alone are reason enough. One of the simplest, highest-impact changes most students can make is putting the phone in another room.

The two things your brain actually needs

Retrieval practice. Every time you pull information out of your brain — rather than putting it in — you make that memory stronger and more durable. Testing yourself, even badly, beats reading perfectly.

Spaced repetition. Reviewing material right before you forget it forces your brain to rebuild the memory stronger. Spreading study sessions across days and weeks creates long-term retention that cramming can’t touch.

Almost every effective study technique is a variation of one or both of these principles. The rest of this guide shows you how to apply them — for different subjects, different kinds of brains, and different constraints on your time.

Chapter 2

The core techniques

These aren’t tips — they’re methods. Most are strongly backed by research (a couple have lighter evidence, flagged where it matters), and each has a specific way to apply it. Learn the technique first, then adapt it to how you study.

1

Active recall (self-testing)

The single most effective study technique for most students and most subjects. Instead of re-reading, you close the book and force yourself to retrieve the information from scratch. Every successful retrieval strengthens the memory. Every failed retrieval shows you exactly what to study.

How to apply it

After reading a section, close everything and write down everything you remember — like a brain dump. Then open the material and check what you missed. Alternatively: make flashcards and quiz yourself; cover your notes and recite out loud; or try answering past exam questions without looking anything up first.

  • Vocabulary
  • History facts
  • Science concepts
  • Math formulas
  • Literature themes

ADHD adaptation

Keep recall sessions short — 10 minutes at most. Use index cards you can physically shuffle and flip. Apps like Anki or Quizlet with gamified streaks can harness the dopamine system. Avoid long self-testing marathons; momentum matters more than duration.

2

Spaced repetition

Reviewing information at increasing intervals exploits the “spacing effect”: memories reviewed just before they fade are rebuilt stronger each time. It’s efficient — more retention for less total study time.

How to apply it

Learn it on Day 1, then review it on Day 2, Day 5, Day 12, and Day 30 — then monthly until the exam. (The review calendar in Building your study system lays this out.) If you’re using flashcards, sort them into piles: “know it,” “shaky,” and “don’t know.” Review “don’t know” every session, “shaky” every two or three days, and “know it” weekly. Anki automates this with an algorithm.

  • Languages
  • Vocabulary
  • Biology terms
  • Historical dates
  • Math formulas

Tip

Pair spaced repetition with active recall and you have the most powerful learning combination known. It’s how many medical students learn thousands of terms — and it works at any level.

3

The Feynman technique

Named after the physicist Richard Feynman, this technique is simple and brutal: if you can’t explain something simply, you don’t actually understand it. It forces you to find the gaps in your understanding — not just the gaps in your memorization. (Researchers call the underlying skill self-explanation: explaining how and why something works, in your own words, is one of the most reliable ways to turn shaky familiarity into real understanding.)

How to apply it

  1. Choose a concept. Pick one topic — photosynthesis, the causes of World War I, how quadratics work.
  2. Explain it to a 10-year-old (or your notebook). Write or speak a full explanation using simple words. No jargon. If you can’t say it simply, you don’t know it yet.
  3. Find where you got stuck. Any place you said “um,” used the textbook term without understanding it, or went blank — that’s what you need to study.
  4. Go back and fix it. Return to your materials specifically to fill the gaps. Then try again.
  • Science concepts
  • Math proofs
  • Historical causation
  • Philosophy
  • Economics
4

Cornell notes

A structured note format whose real value is what it makes you do: put ideas in your own words, then test yourself on them. The layout isn’t magic — the gains come from this generative processing (reformulating and self-quizzing), not from the columns themselves. Done as mindless copying, Cornell notes help no more than any other notes.

How to apply it

Divide your page into three areas: a narrow cue column on the left (about 2.5 inches), a wide notes area on the right, and a summary box at the bottom (about 2 inches). During class or reading, write notes in the right column. Afterward, write questions in the left column that your notes answer. At the bottom, write a two- or three-sentence summary in your own words. To review, cover the notes and use the questions to quiz yourself.

  • Lectures
  • Textbooks
  • History
  • Science

ADHD adaptation

Cornell can feel rigid. Modify it: use a two-column sticky-note system instead, or go digital with Notion or OneNote, where you can add tags. The key is the question column — even if the layout is looser, adding questions to your notes transforms them.

5

The Pomodoro technique

A time-management method that breaks study into structured intervals. It works because it makes the task feel finite, forces you to protect focus time, and builds in the breaks your brain needs to consolidate what it just processed. It protects your attention; the learning itself still comes from active techniques, like recall, inside each block.

How to apply it

The standard version: 25 minutes of focused work, a 5-minute break, repeat four times, then a longer 20–30-minute break. During the 25 minutes, do nothing but the task — phone in another room, one tab open. After four cycles (“pomodoros”), do something genuinely restorative: walk, eat, stretch — not scroll.

Works best for students who procrastinate, long writing assignments, math problem sets, and anyone who loses track of time.

Watch out if you hit flow states (interrupting deep focus can be counterproductive), or if you tend to use the timer as permission to put off starting.

ADHD adaptation: a modified Pomodoro

The standard 25/5 split doesn’t work for every ADHD brain. Try 10/5 (10 minutes of work, 5 of rest) to start, and build up as you succeed. The goal is starting, not duration. Also try body-doubling: work alongside someone else, in person or on a virtual co-working stream — the social context helps engage executive function.

6

Mind mapping

A visual technique for showing how concepts connect. Unlike linear notes, a mind map radiates out from a central idea, making relationships and structure visible at a glance. It’s best for understanding how things fit together, not for memorizing individual facts.

How to apply it

Write the central topic in the middle of a blank page. Draw branches out to the main subtopics. Add smaller branches for details, examples, or connections. Use color, symbols, and drawings — the more distinctive, the more memorable. Don’t just copy your notes; force yourself to reorganize them into a structure that makes sense to you.

  • Essay planning
  • History overviews
  • Biology systems
  • Review summaries

Going further

Use a different color for each branch, sketch icons instead of words, and create a master overview map at the start of each unit, updating it as you learn more. To show how ideas relate (not just cluster), label the lines between them — “causes,” “depends on.” A map with labeled links (a “concept map”) forces deeper thinking than a plain web.

Caution

The evidence for mind mapping is weaker than for active recall or spacing, and it’s easy to spend an hour making something beautiful rather than learned. Keep it fast and rough, and always finish by closing the map and rebuilding it from memory — that retrieval is where the learning actually happens.

7

Interleaving (mixing practice)

Most students study one topic at a time until they’ve mastered it (“blocked practice”). Research shows that deliberately mixing problem types or topics — even when it feels harder — produces better long-term retention and transfer.

How to apply it

Instead of doing 20 quadratic equations and then 20 factoring problems, mix them: problem 1 (quadratic), problem 2 (factoring), problem 3 (quadratic), and so on. In history, rotate between cause-and-effect questions, timeline questions, and primary-source analysis. It’ll feel harder — that’s the point. Difficulty during practice means durability in memory.

  • Math
  • Science problems
  • Languages
  • Test prep

Common mistake

Interleaving is not the same as randomly bouncing between subjects every few minutes. You still need to understand the fundamentals of each topic before mixing. Think of it as practice for exam conditions, where you won’t be told which method to use.

8

Elaborative interrogation (ask “why?”)

A deceptively simple technique: for every fact you’re trying to learn, ask why it’s true and generate an explanation. This connects new information to things you already know, creating a richer memory network that’s far easier to retrieve.

How to apply it

Read a fact: “Mitosis produces two genetically identical cells.” Ask: why? (To replace cells without changing the genetic blueprint.) Why does that matter? (A skin cell replacing itself needs the same genetic instructions.) Keep asking until you’ve linked it to something you already understand. Write the chain of whys in the margin.

  • Biology
  • Chemistry
  • Historical causation
  • Economics
  • Physics
9

SQ3R (active reading)

SQ3R turns passive reading into an active process. Most students read textbooks start to finish without retaining much. SQ3R builds questions before you read, forcing your brain to search for answers rather than passively absorbing words.

How to apply it

  • Survey: skim the headings, bold terms, diagrams, and summary before reading. Get the map.
  • Question: turn each heading into a question. “Types of Chemical Bonds” becomes “What are the types of chemical bonds, and how do they differ?”
  • Read to answer your questions.
  • Recite: after each section, look away and answer your question from memory.
  • Review: at the end, summarize the whole chapter in your own words.
  • Textbook reading
  • Science chapters
  • History texts

Honest caveat

As a full five-step package, SQ3R has surprisingly little direct research behind it. The steps that carry the weight are the well-proven ones: turning headings into questions and reciting from memory are forms of self-testing. If the full ritual feels like too much, just do 3R — read, recite, review: read a section, look away and say it back, then check. In experiments with college students, 3R led to better recall than either re-reading or note-taking, and it took less time than taking notes (McDaniel, Howard & Einstein, 2009).

10

The Blurt (brain dump)

The simplest active recall technique there is. Take a blank piece of paper, write the topic at the top, and then write down everything you know about it — without looking at anything. Everything. Then check what you missed and study only those gaps.

How to apply it

Before a test, blurt everything you know about “The French Revolution” or “Chapter 5: Cell Division.” What comes out easily is consolidated. What you struggle with or forget completely is exactly what needs work. Repeat after filling the gaps until the page is full. This works as a self-diagnostic as much as a learning technique.

  • Pre-test review
  • Any subject
  • Quick check-ins

ADHD adaptation

The Blurt is perfect for ADHD — it’s open-ended, time-limited, and produces something tangible. Set a timer for 5–7 minutes. Speak it out loud instead of writing if that’s easier, and record a voice note. Movement version: pace while you blurt out loud.

11

Dual coding (words and visuals)

Pair what you’re learning with a relevant picture: a diagram, sketch, timeline, flowchart, or quick doodle. Storing an idea as both words and an image gives your memory two independent routes back to it — so if one fails on test day, the other can still fire. This is the real, evidence-based reason visual study tools work, and it helps every student, not a “type.”

How to apply it

As you take notes, add a small drawing or diagram beside key ideas — keep it rough; meaning beats art. Turn a process into a flowchart, a comparison into a table, a trend into a sketched graph. Then cover the words and explain the picture; cover the picture and redraw it from the words. Don’t just decorate — the image has to carry real meaning.

  • Biology processes
  • History timelines
  • Physics and chemistry
  • Geography
  • Vocabulary
12

Pretesting (guess before you learn)

Try to answer questions on a topic before you’ve studied it — and don’t worry about getting them wrong. The act of guessing, then seeing the right answer, makes that answer far stickier than reading it cold. Being wrong first actually primes your brain to grab the correct information when it arrives.

How to apply it

Before reading a new chapter, attempt the end-of-chapter questions, or turn each heading into a question and take a guess. Then check your answers as you read. It feels uncomfortable to attempt things you “haven’t learned yet” — that discomfort is the technique working.

  • New topics
  • Textbook chapters
  • Science and math
  • Any subject with practice questions
13

Concrete examples

Abstract ideas are slippery; examples give them something to stick to. When you’re learning a concept, collect two or three concrete, real examples of it — ideally from different contexts, so your brain latches onto the underlying idea rather than the surface details of a single case.

How to apply it

For each concept, write down a couple of specific examples in your own words — then try to invent one yourself that wasn’t in the book (that’s the real test of understanding). It works in reverse too: gather several examples and figure out the principle that connects them. Vary the examples so you don’t tie the idea to one situation.

  • Abstract concepts
  • Economics
  • Physics
  • Definitions
  • Essay arguments
14

Self-explanation

The close cousin of the Feynman technique — but aimed at process rather than a whole topic. As you work through a solved problem or a passage, pause at each step and explain to yourself why this step, and why now. It turns silent reading or copying into active sense-making, and it surfaces the steps you were secretly taking on faith.

How to apply it

Work through a worked example one line at a time. At each line, ask: “Why is this step here? What rule makes it valid? How does it follow from the line before?” Say or write the answer. When reading, after each paragraph explain how it connects to what came before. Anywhere you can’t explain the “why” is a gap to go fix.

  • Math worked examples
  • Physics and chemistry
  • Computer science
  • Dense readings

Chapter 3

Matching techniques to subjects

Different subjects put different demands on your brain. Math is not English is not chemistry. Here’s how to use techniques strategically — and why some methods that work brilliantly for one subject fail for another.

Mathematics

  • Practice problems — always
  • Interleaving (mix problem types)
  • Worked examples, then cover and redo
  • The Feynman technique for proofs
  • An error log: redo every problem you got wrong

Avoid flashcards and re-reading notes. Math is learned by doing, not watching.

Sciences

  • Diagrams, with labeled redrawing from memory
  • Active recall on terminology (flashcards)
  • Elaborative interrogation (“why does this reaction happen?”)
  • Worked past-paper questions
  • Concept mapping to link systems

Best combination: Cornell notes, active recall, and a mind map for the overview.

History

  • Timelines (visual and sequential)
  • Cause-and-effect webs (a mind-map variant)
  • Active recall of dates and events
  • Practice essays, written without notes
  • Perspective cards: “who believed what, and why?”

Best combination: Cornell notes for reading, the Blurt for review, then practice essays.

English and literature

  • Quote banks (active recall of key quotes)
  • Character and theme diagrams
  • Essay planning (structure before writing)
  • Reading aloud for rhythm and voice
  • Argument mapping: thesis, evidence, analysis

Avoid re-reading the text passively. Analyze as you read.

Foreign languages

  • Spaced repetition for vocabulary (Anki is ideal)
  • Shadowing: listen and repeat aloud
  • Active recall: translate without looking
  • Writing sentences with new vocabulary (context beats isolation)
  • Music and podcasts in the target language

Best combination: Anki daily, plus writing and speaking aloud.

Computer science

  • Code from scratch (no copy-paste learning)
  • Rubber-duck debugging (explain your code to an object)
  • Trace through code by hand to check the logic
  • The Feynman technique for algorithms
  • Active recall of syntax and concepts

Avoid watching tutorials without coding alongside. Passive watching creates false familiarity.

Geography and social studies

  • Map labeling (draw and label from memory)
  • Case-study cards: location, facts, significance
  • Cause-and-effect diagrams
  • Data and statistics recall cards

Best combination: visual maps plus case-study active recall.

Music and the arts

  • Spaced practice (30 minutes daily beats three hours on the weekend)
  • Record yourself, then listen critically
  • Active recall for theory terms
  • Break pieces into sections and master each one

Consistency beats duration. Daily practice builds motor and auditory memory.

When a technique isn’t working, switch it up

Not every technique suits every student, even in the same subject. Here’s a quick swap guide.

If flashcards aren’t sticking:

  • Put the fact in a story or analogy instead
  • Use image-based cards instead of text-only ones
  • Speak the answer out loud instead of reading it
  • Switch from solo review to quizzing a friend

If mind maps feel chaotic:

  • Try a simple outline first
  • Use a table: concept, definition, example, connection
  • Try Cornell notes instead if you think more linearly

If practice problems aren’t improving your scores:

  • You’re doing, not learning: after each problem, explain why the method works
  • Keep an error log, and practice only what you got wrong
  • Study worked examples first, then attempt problems on your own

If active recall feels impossible:

  • Start with the book open, and glance at it less and less over time
  • Try prompt-based recall: cover everything except a single keyword
  • Do a Blurt with a friend — talking makes retrieval feel less high-stakes

Chapter 4

Studying in multiple formats

You’ll often hear people call themselves a “visual learner” or an “auditory learner.” It’s a comfortable idea — but matching study to a fixed personal “style” hasn’t held up when researchers tested it (Pashler et al., 2008). Two things are true and useful. First, some material suits a particular format — a map wants to be seen, a poem wants to be heard. Second, encoding the same idea in several formats builds stronger, more retrievable memories for everyone. So treat the sections below not as “your type,” but as a toolbox: match the tool to the content, and stack a few formats together for anything important.

The honest truth

A format preference is fine as a way to get started — if diagrams pull you in, start there. Just don’t stop there. The student who draws a concept and says it aloud and writes an example will out-remember the one who only draws. Preference affects motivation; multi-format encoding affects memory. Use the first to start and the second to learn.

Visual formats

Best for content with structure, relationships, or a sequence you can see.

Reach for these when the material is spatial or graphical — processes, hierarchies, comparisons, anything where seeing the relationships makes them click. They’re useful to everyone, especially as the visual half of dual coding.

  • Mind maps and concept webs — especially for complex, interconnected material
  • Color-coded notes — assign colors to categories, not at random (blue for causes, red for effects, green for key terms)
  • Sketch-noting — combine small drawings, symbols, and text in the margins
  • Flowcharts and process diagrams — excellent for biological processes, historical sequences, and algorithms
  • Timelines drawn spatially rather than listed
  • Flashcards with images — add a small, relevant sketch to each card
  • Cover-and-redraw — study a diagram, then reproduce it from memory

Setting: a clean, visually uncluttered space. Whiteboards are ideal; standing and writing large gives you room to lay the ideas out.

Auditory formats

Best for language, argument, and anything that lives in sound or speech.

Reach for these when you can put the material into sound — explaining out loud, discussing, or hearing it. Speaking an idea also doubles as active recall, which is why “teach it aloud” is so powerful for anyone.

  • Read your notes out loud — your own voice leaves a more memorable trace than silent reading
  • Explain concepts to a friend or a recording device — teach it, and you’ll hear whether you really know it
  • Record yourself summarizing a topic, then play it back while walking or eating
  • Study groups and discussions — talking about material beats reading it alone
  • Mnemonic rhymes and verbal patterns — rhythm aids recall (think of how easily song lyrics stick)
  • Podcast-style review — some subjects have audio resources; use them
  • The Feynman technique, spoken aloud — explaining verbally is especially powerful

Setting: some people concentrate better with background music or ambient sound than in silence. If you use music, keep it instrumental during intensive work.

Hands-on formats

Best for procedures, skills, and abstract ideas that need to be made concrete.

Reach for these when sitting and reading isn’t sticking, or when the material is a procedure or a skill. Acting things out, building models, and moving while you review can boost focus and motivation — and turning an abstract idea into something physical makes it concrete.

  • Walk-and-review — pace while reviewing flashcards or talking through a topic
  • Build models or use physical objects — 3D molecule models for chemistry, a physical timeline on the floor
  • Role-play and dramatize — act out historical events, simulate scientific processes
  • Write by hand — handwriting is slower than typing, so it forces you to summarize in your own words instead of transcribing word for word (that processing is the real benefit, not the physical act itself)
  • Whiteboard standing sessions — standing and writing large keeps you moving
  • Manipulatives for math — physical counters, geometry tools, graph paper
  • Study games — Quizlet Live, Kahoot-style quizzes, competitive flashcard drills
  • Cook, build, or create to learn — apply chemistry to cooking, physics to sports mechanics

Setting: don’t fight the urge to move. A standing desk, pacing, or changing locations can all help if sitting still for hours drains your focus.

Text-based formats

Best for definitions, detailed arguments, and anything you’ll be assessed on in writing.

Reach for these when precise wording matters, or when the exam itself is written. The trap with text methods is that copying feels productive — so make writing generative: reformulate in your own words and test yourself from it, rather than transcribing.

  • Thorough Cornell notes — the structure suits text-heavy material well
  • Rewriting notes in your own words — not copying verbatim, but reformulating
  • Written Feynman explanations — write the explanation as if it’s a guide for someone else
  • Summarizing chapters without looking — write your version, then compare
  • Glossaries and definition banks that you build yourself, not copy from the book
  • Practice essays with strict time limits — writing under pressure is its own skill

Pitfall: the urge to make perfect, clean notes can turn into procrastination. Making notes is not studying. Reviewing and testing yourself from your notes is studying.

Stacking formats for stronger memory

This is the part that’s backed by strong evidence: the most resilient memories are encoded in more than one format. Whatever you reach for first, adding another — say it aloud, sketch it, write an example, act it out — creates more retrieval pathways, so you can reach the memory from more angles on test day.

The multi-encoding stack

For any important concept: draw it, then say it out loud, then write an example, then teach it to someone. Four encodings, four routes back to the memory. One will work when you need it.

Chapter 5

Neurodivergent learners

Standard study advice is written for a mythical average student. If your brain works differently — whether because of ADHD, dyslexia, anxiety, autism, or another profile — much of that advice will feel either impossible or actively counterproductive. This section gives honest, specific modifications that work with your neurology, not against it.

A word before you start

None of these profiles is a monolith. ADHD looks wildly different from one student to the next; autism presents differently in different people. Use the relevant section as a starting menu of ideas to experiment with — not as a prescription. You’re the expert on your own brain.

ADHDStudying with an interest-based nervous system

ADHD brains don’t regulate attention on command — they regulate it through interest, urgency, challenge, and novelty. So “just try harder to focus” isn’t a technique; it’s not how the underlying system works. The goal is to engineer conditions that naturally engage ADHD attention.

  • Gamify everything you can. Anki streaks, Quizlet flashcard races, timed challenges, bets with yourself. The artificial urgency of a timer engages ADHD attention in a way that “I should study this” does not.
  • Body doubling. Work alongside someone else — a friend, a parent, a virtual co-working stream. The social presence engages executive function without requiring you to talk to each other.
  • Short, very defined sessions. Ten to fifteen minutes with a specific task (“I will do 10 flashcards and then stop”) beats open-ended “study time.” The finite endpoint makes it easier to start.
  • Movement. Pace while reviewing. Use a standing desk or an exercise ball. Walk and listen to recorded notes. Physical activity raises dopamine and norepinephrine — the same brain chemicals involved in ADHD.
  • Anchor habits. Study at the same time, in the same place, with the same starting ritual (same music, same drink, same first task). Fewer startup decisions means less friction.
  • Written task lists with micro-tasks. Instead of “study biology,” write “draw the cell-cycle diagram without looking.” Check it off. The specificity reduces the executive-function load of figuring out what to do.
  • Work with hyperfocus, not against it. If you lock onto a topic unexpectedly, ride it — don’t switch away because it’s not next on the schedule. Hyperfocus is a superpower when it’s aimed at relevant material.
  • Reduce visual clutter. One notebook, one pen, phone in another room. Every extra object is a potential distraction.

Avoid or modify: long uninterrupted study sessions; passive re-reading; studying with social media open; self-criticism for struggling to start (difficulty starting tasks is neurological, not a character flaw).

DyslexiaBuilding on strengths in reasoning and creativity

Dyslexia affects reading speed and decoding, not intelligence or reasoning ability. Many dyslexic students are strong conceptual thinkers who are held back by text-heavy study methods — the answer is to reduce the decoding load while keeping the thinking demanding.

  • Text-to-speech tools. Read&Write, NaturalReader, or your computer’s built-in reader can read textbooks, notes, and web pages aloud. Use it actively, not as a crutch — listen, engage, and take your own notes as you go.
  • Audio recordings of lectures. Don’t try to write and process at the same time — record now, process later. Your notes can be sparse during class; fill them in from the recording.
  • Readable formatting. Many dyslexic readers find plain sans-serif fonts, such as Arial or Verdana, easier to read. Special “dyslexia fonts” haven’t shown a clear benefit in studies, so use one only if you find it more comfortable. Use a font size of 14 or larger, line spacing of 1.5 or double, and a cream or tinted background instead of bright white.
  • Visual and spatial methods. Mind maps, diagrams, and timelines reduce the need for fluent text reading — lean on them.
  • Oral practice. If your school allows spoken answers on assessments, practice explaining answers out loud as well as in writing.
  • Chunk your reading. Read one paragraph at a time with a specific question in mind. Don’t try to absorb large blocks at once.
  • Extra time is not an advantage — it’s equity. If you’re entitled to extended time on exams, use it strategically. Practice under normal conditions too, so it’s a safety net, not a dependency.

Avoid or modify: handwritten note-taking without an audio backup; timed reading exercises that create anxiety; expecting the same reading speed as non-dyslexic classmates.

Academic anxietyReducing threat, building confidence

Anxiety activates the body’s threat response, which impairs the prefrontal cortex — the part of the brain that handles working memory and focus. Studying under high anxiety is inefficient, so the study environment itself is part of the fix.

  • Low-stakes recall first. Start sessions with material you already know well — warm up retrieval before challenging yourself. Starting with failure can trigger a spiral.
  • Separate practice from performance. Practice tests are for learning, not judgment. Write “PRACTICE” at the top of every practice paper to signal to yourself that this isn’t being evaluated.
  • Box breathing before you study. Breathe in for 4 seconds, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4 — two or three cycles. Slow breathing engages your “rest” (parasympathetic) system and can quickly take the edge off acute stress, which frees up working memory for studying. (The popular claim that it “lowers cortisol” is shakier than it sounds; what’s reliable is that it calms you down, and a calmer brain learns better.)
  • Break catastrophic thinking. When the thought is “I don’t know anything,” do a Blurt — write down everything you do know. The evidence on paper counters the story in your head.
  • Small sessions, lots of wins. Anxiety feeds on helplessness and overwhelm. Micro-sessions with achievable goals (“learn 10 vocabulary words today”) build confidence faster than marathon sessions that end in burnout.
  • Focus on process, not outcome. “I will spend 25 minutes on this,” not “I need to know this perfectly.” Process goals are achievable; outcome goals create anxiety.

Avoid: studying in a space you associate with anxiety or failure; checking practice-test scores over and over mid-session; comparing notes with classmates right before an exam.

AutismLeveraging depth, managing overload

Autistic students often have extraordinary depth of focus in areas of interest, strong systemizing skills, and excellent pattern recognition — but may find context-switching, implicit instructions, and the sensory side of study environments harder.

  • Use special interests as bridges. Connect course content to an area of deep interest wherever you can. Motivation and retention in a topic of intense interest can be exceptional — use them.
  • Explicit structure over implicit expectations. Ambiguous tasks (“discuss this topic”) are harder than explicit ones (“write three causes and three effects as bullet points”). If an assignment is unclear, ask for the structure it requires.
  • Systems and frameworks first. Before the details, get the system: the rules, the categories, the logic. It’s often easier to slot details into a framework you already own.
  • Manage the sensory environment. Noise-canceling headphones, consistent lighting, familiar textures, a comfortable temperature. Sensory discomfort competes directly with thinking — removing it isn’t indulgence, it’s function.
  • Written instructions. Keep a written record of what you need to study and when. Spoken instructions can be harder to retain — photograph the board, ask for written summaries.
  • Transition warnings between tasks. Build explicit transition rituals: a five-minute warning timer, closing the notebook, standing up, a two-minute movement break. Abrupt switches between topics are harder; plan them.
  • Routine-based scheduling. Studying at the same times in the same places isn’t rigidity — it’s an efficient strategy. The environmental cues reduce executive-function overhead.

DyscalculiaWorking with number-processing differences

Dyscalculia affects numerical processing and spatial-mathematical reasoning, not general intelligence. Students with dyscalculia often have strong verbal and creative abilities that can be put to work.

  • Say math procedures out loud. Narrate each step as you work through a problem. The verbal encoding compensates for weaker numerical encoding.
  • Color-code number systems. Use different colors for different operations, place values, or kinds of numbers. Visual differences reduce confusion.
  • Physical manipulatives. Base-10 blocks, counting objects, graph paper for geometry — concrete before abstract.
  • Calculators for computation, not understanding. Use tools that lighten the calculation load, so your effort goes to the concepts.
  • Anchor numbers to real situations. Abstract numbers are harder to process; tie them to concrete scenarios that mean something to you.

Chapter 6

Building your study system

Knowing techniques is useless without a system that puts them into practice. A study system answers three questions: what do I study, when do I study it, and how do I know it’s working?

Step 1

Weekly planning

At the start of each week, identify exactly what needs to be done. Not “study for biology” — specifics like “complete active recall cards for Chapter 6” or “write three practice essay paragraphs on the causes of World War II.” Vague goals create decision fatigue and procrastination.

The three-category weekly list

Divide your list into Must do (assessed material, upcoming tests), Should do (review to consolidate), and Want to do (deeper exploration). Start every session with Must do. Never start with Want to do.

Turn plans into if–then triggers

A vague intention (“I’ll review biology this week”) is easy to skip. Tie each task to a specific time and place instead: “If it’s 4 p.m. on Tuesday, then I’ll do 10 Anki cards at my desk.” These if–then plans — researchers call them implementation intentions — reliably boost follow-through, because the moment itself becomes the cue and you don’t have to decide all over again.

ADHD version

Compress each Pomodoro to 15 minutes, and add a physical-activity break (not a screen break) between every block. Plan one specific micro-task per block, written out before you start. Use a visual timer you can see counting down — invisible timers don’t work as well for ADHD brains.

Step 3

The review calendar

For material you genuinely need to retain (not just survive the next quiz), plan your reviews in advance.

  1. Day 1Learn it
  2. Day 2Review (active recall)
  3. Day 5Review again
  4. Day 12Review
  5. Day 30Review
  6. After thatMonthly until the exam

What counts as a review? Ten flashcard reps on the topic, one Blurt without looking, a Feynman explanation to yourself, one past-paper question on the material, or teaching it to a classmate.

If the test comes sooner than the calendar allows, squeeze it: a short retrieval session every day until the test, plus a quick warm-up on the day itself. Afterward, restart the calendar for anything you need to keep.

Step 4

How to know if it’s working

The only reliable test: can you produce the information without looking at your notes? Not “does it feel familiar?” — familiarity is a feeling, not knowledge. Knowledge is retrieval.

Why you can’t trust the “I know this” feeling

Re-reading and highlighting make text feel easy and familiar, and your brain mistakes that fluency for actual mastery. It’s called the fluency illusion, and it’s why students who “felt ready” still bomb the test. The cure is to judge by evidence, not by feel: the only way to know what you can retrieve is to try to retrieve it. That’s what makes self-testing your honest mirror, not just a study method.

Use these checkpoints:

  • The weekly Blurt test. At the end of each week, take a blank page and write down everything you can recall from that week’s material. Nothing open, no prompts. What fills the page is learned; what’s missing is next week’s priority.
  • Past-paper scoring. Do one past exam question per major topic each month, under real conditions. Don’t grade it leniently — compare your answer to the answer key or rubric, or have a teacher check it. This is the most honest signal you have.
  • The teaching test. Explain a topic to a younger sibling, a parent who doesn’t know the subject, or an imaginary student. The places where you stumble, get vague, or skip over are exactly your gaps.
  • Adjust without judgment. If something isn’t working after two weeks of honest effort, change the technique — not the verdict on yourself. Studying well is a process of experimenting and adjusting, not passing or failing.

Step 5

None of these are required — paper and a pen beat any app you don’t actually use. But the right tool can lower the friction of starting and automate the boring parts, like scheduling reviews.

The tool trap

Setting up the perfect app, color scheme, and template can become a sophisticated form of procrastination. Pick one tool per job, spend 10 minutes setting it up, and start studying. You can refine it later.

Step 6

Protect your attention

The best technique in the world fails if your attention is fragmented. For most students this is one of the highest-leverage changes there is — bigger than any single method.

Your phone:

  • Put it in another room, not just face-down — out of reach means nothing to glance at and nothing to resist.
  • Turn notifications off (or use Do Not Disturb) during every focus block.
  • No phone during breaks either — scrolling fragments the downtime your brain uses to consolidate.

Single-tasking:

  • One tab, one task. Each “quick check” of messages costs a slow mental reload back into the work.
  • Bouncing between subjects mid-thought isn’t interleaving — it’s just task-switching, and it hurts.
  • Park stray thoughts on a scrap “later” list so you don’t act on them now.

Rest, sleep, and wellbeing

Sleep is not the enemy of studying — it is studying. Much of the rewiring that turns today’s practice into lasting memory happens while you sleep, especially during slow-wave and REM sleep, so a short night throws away part of what you studied. A 7-hour night is worth more to your exam performance than an extra 2 hours of tired reviewing. Students who are consistently short on sleep have measurably worse recall, focus, and emotional regulation — and worse exam results, no matter how many hours they “studied.”

Non-negotiable foundations

Sleep 7–9 hours. Eat something before studying. Take real breaks. Exercise during the week — even 20-minute walks help memory and focus. These aren’t soft self-care tips. They’re cognitive performance strategies.

The one-page cheat sheet

Everything that matters, condensed.

The whole guide in one sentence

Stop putting information in (re-reading, highlighting) and start pulling it out (testing yourself), spread across days, with your phone in another room — then sleep on it.

The 14 techniques at a glance

  1. Active recallClose the book and retrieve from memory. The number-one technique for almost everything.
  2. Spaced repetitionReview at growing intervals. Paired with active recall, it’s hard to beat.
  3. The Feynman techniqueExplain it simply; the gaps are wherever you stall.
  4. Cornell notesCue column, notes, and summary, with review built into the format.
  5. Pomodoro25 minutes on, 5 off (or 10/5 for ADHD).
  6. Mind mappingA radial map of how ideas connect.
  7. InterleavingMix problem types; harder means better.
  8. Ask “why?”Link every fact to something you already know.
  9. SQ3RSurvey, question, read, recite, review (or just the 3R core).
  10. The BlurtBrain-dump a topic, then fix the gaps.
  11. Dual codingPair words with a picture or diagram; two memory routes beat one.
  12. PretestingGuess before you learn; being wrong first primes you for the right answer.
  13. Concrete examplesPin abstract ideas to two or three real cases.
  14. Self-explanationAt each step, ask “why this, why now?”

Stop doing, start doing

Stop doing

  • Re-reading notes
  • Highlighting as if it’s studying
  • Cramming the night before
  • Marathon single sessions
  • Watching tutorials without doing
  • Studying with your phone in reach

Start doing

  • Testing yourself from memory
  • Reviewing on Day 1, 2, 5, 12, and 30
  • Short, focused blocks with real breaks
  • Explaining things out loud
  • Sleeping 7–9 hours
  • Putting your phone in another room

The review calendar

  1. Day 1Learn it
  2. Day 2Review (active recall)
  3. Day 5Review again
  4. Day 12Review
  5. Day 30Review
  6. After thatMonthly until the exam

Learn it on Day 1, then review on Day 2, Day 5, Day 12, and Day 30 — then monthly until the exam. A review can be 10 flashcards, a Blurt, a Feynman explanation, or one past-paper question.

Is it actually working?

One test: can you produce it without looking? Familiarity is a feeling; knowledge is retrieval. If a technique isn’t working after two honest weeks, change the technique — not your verdict on yourself.
